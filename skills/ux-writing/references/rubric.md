# The rubric

Four standards, applied as passes in this order. A string that fails an
earlier pass is not polished by a later one: a concise sentence that
serves no purpose is still waste.

The standards are Torrey Podmajersky's (*Strategic Writing for UX*,
O'Reilly, 2nd edition 2025). The check questions below are this skill's
restatement, not the book's text.

## 1. Purposeful — does it need to exist?

- What does the person need from this string, at this moment, to do the
  thing they came to do?
- Does it serve their goal, or the product's wish to be noticed?
- Would they miss it if it were gone? Text nobody misses is removed, not
  rewritten.
- Is it the only place this is said? Two strings saying one thing is one
  too many.

## 2. Concise — is every word earning its place?

- Front-load: the word the person scans for comes first.
- Cut "please", "simply", "just", "in order to", "successfully", and
  restated context ("You can now...").
- One idea per sentence; one sentence where one will do.
- Shorter is a means, not the goal. A short string that leaves out the
  fix is worse than a longer one that includes it.

## 3. Conversational — does it sound like the product talking to a person?

- Active voice and a named actor, unless the actor is irrelevant.
- The person's words, not the system's: "Couldn't save", not
  "Persistence failure".
- The voice from Voice & terms, matched in tone to the stakes. Warmth
  where things went wrong, never jokes where money, data or access is at
  risk.
- Read it aloud. If it sounds like a form, a lawyer or an advert, it is
  not done.

## 4. Clear — can it be misread?

- One name per concept, the one in the glossary.
- Specific over general: "Delete 3 invoices", not "Delete items".
- No jargon, code, or abbreviation the reader has not been given.
- Plain words at the reading level of the audience.
- Says what happens next, when anything does.

## Guidance numbers, not law

These figures circulate in UX-writing practice and are collected in the
MIT-licensed [content-designer/ux-writing-skill](https://github.com/content-designer/ux-writing-skill).
They were **not traced to primary studies** for this skill. Use them to
notice an outlier worth a second look, never as a pass mark, and never
quote them to a user as research findings.

| Thing | Commonly cited range |
|---|---|
| Button label | 2 to 4 words |
| Error message, fix included | about 12 to 18 words |
| Line length for reading | about 40 to 60 characters |
| Reading level, general audience | around school grade 7 |
| Reading level, professional audience | around school grade 10 |

## Scoring findings

- Score findings, not strings. A string that passes every standard gets
  no line in the report.
- Rank by what the failure costs the reader: blocked (cannot proceed or
  recover), misled (proceeds wrongly), slowed (proceeds after effort),
  irritated (proceeds, likes the product less).
- Most products have a handful of blocked or misled findings and a long
  tail of slowed ones. A report where everything is critical has not
  ranked anything.
- A finding is a broken rule with a cost. "I would have phrased it
  differently" is not a finding.
